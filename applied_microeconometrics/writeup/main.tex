\question{Objective of the reference paper and the role of panel data}

Xu, Saksena and Holly (2011) study what drives national health expenditure and
how it evolves, motivated by the scarcity of evidence outside the OECD. The aim
is to understand the trajectory of health expenditure in developing countries,
using 143 countries over 1995 to 2008. Four research questions organise the
study:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Does total health expenditure grow faster than income?
  \item What other factors are associated with that growth?
  \item Do government and out-of-pocket expenditure follow the same path?
  \item Do countries at different income levels follow the same trend?
\end{enumerate}

The first is central: whether the income elasticity exceeds unity, that is,
whether health care is a luxury good or a necessity.

Panel data suits this objective for three reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{It controls for unobserved country heterogeneity.} Health
        system design, institutional quality and relative medical prices are
        unobserved, time-invariant and correlated with income. A cross-section
        leaves them in the error term, so the income elasticity suffers omitted
        variable bias. Panel data removes them through the within
        transformation, and lets us test whether they are present rather than
        assume it.

  \item \textbf{It identifies the elasticity from within-country variation.}
        Between-country variation asks whether richer countries spend more;
        within-country variation asks whether a country spends more as it grows.
        Only the latter describes a trajectory. Time dummies additionally absorb
        common time effects, separating a secular trend in health spending from
        the income effect.

  \item \textbf{It improves efficiency.} Income, population ageing and disease
        burden are collinear across countries. Pooling both dimensions raises the
        degrees of freedom and the independent variation in the regressors,
        reducing multicollinearity and the standard errors.
\end{enumerate}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\question{How this analysis differs from the reference paper}

This analysis departs from the reference paper in three ways, and narrows its
scope in two.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A modern window under current accounting standards.} 2010 to
        2021 under the System of Health Accounts 2011, against 1995 to 2008 under
        WHO's earlier National Health Accounts framework, which followed SHA 1.0.
        The paper's conclusion that health expenditure does not grow faster than
        income was established on a period of uninterrupted growth, closing
        before the financial crisis reached health budgets and long before
        COVID-19. The later window tests whether it survives the largest shock to
        health spending on record.

  \item \textbf{A measure of non-communicable disease burden.} The paper proxies
        disease pattern by tuberculosis incidence alone, and states this was a
        constraint, not a choice: NCD time series were unavailable. This
        analysis adds NCD mortality, the probability that a 30-year-old dies
        before the age of 70 from cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes or
        chronic respiratory disease. TB captures only the infectious side of the
        epidemiological transition, and as countries grow richer the burden
        shifts to chronic conditions. Omitting them biases the income
        coefficient, since NCD burden is itself correlated with income.

  \item \textbf{Two redefined variables.} Fiscal capacity is measured as
        government final consumption as a share of GDP rather than total
        government expenditure, and the demographic variable is the population
        aged 65 and above rather than 60 and above. Both follow from the coverage
        of the modern series, and are defined in Question 4.
\end{enumerate}

Scope narrows in two respects: only total health expenditure is modelled, so
research question 3 is not addressed; and estimation is confined to pooled OLS,
fixed effects and random effects, as the assignment specifies, whereas the paper
adds a Blundell and Bond dynamic estimator.

\question{Dependent and independent variables}

The dependent variable is the logarithm of real current health expenditure per
capita, in constant 2021 PPP international dollars.

The independent variables are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Log real GDP per capita}, constant 2021 PPP international
        dollars. Capacity to pay.

  \item \textbf{Government final consumption expenditure, per cent of GDP.}
        Fiscal capacity, since governments finance a large share of health
        spending.

  \item \textbf{Population aged 65 and above, per cent of total.} Older
        populations consume more health services per head.

  \item \textbf{Log tuberculosis incidence per 100,000.} Infectious disease
        burden.

  \item \textbf{NCD mortality, per cent.} Chronic disease burden, as defined in
        Question 2.

  \item \textbf{World Bank income group}, time-invariant with four levels,
        capturing structural differences in health systems across levels of
        development. Being time-invariant, it is absorbed by the country effect
        under fixed effects and is identified only in the pooled and random
        effects models.
\end{enumerate}

The base specification is

\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathit{CHE}_{it} = {} & \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln \mathit{GDP}_{it}
  + \beta_2 \mathit{GFCE}_{it} + \beta_3 \mathit{OVER65}_{it} \\
  & + \beta_4 \ln \mathit{TB}_{it} + \beta_5 \mathit{NCD}_{it}
  + \gamma' \mathit{INC}_i + u_i + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

where $u_i$ is the unobserved country effect.

The log transformation matters for three reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Expenditure and income enter in logs so that $\beta_1$ is the income
        elasticity directly, reducing research question 1 to a test of
        $H_0 : \beta_1 = 1$.

  \item Expenditure, income and TB incidence are strongly right-skewed and span
        several orders of magnitude across countries. Logs linearise the
        relationship and stabilise the error variance.

  \item Variables already expressed as percentages enter in levels, where their
        coefficients read as the effect of a one percentage point change.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{PPP and constant prices.} WHO publishes health expenditure only on a
current-price PPP basis, while GDP per capita is available on both current and
constant bases. Health expenditure is therefore rebased to constant 2021
international dollars by multiplying it by the ratio of constant to current GDP
per capita in PPP:

\begin{equation*}
\mathit{CHE}^{\,real}_{it}
  = \mathit{CHE}^{\,current}_{it}
    \times \frac{\mathit{GDP}^{\,constant}_{it}}{\mathit{GDP}^{\,current}_{it}}
\end{equation*}

This ratio is the country-year deflator implied by the World Bank's own real-GDP
construction, so expenditure and income end in the same units and $\beta_1$ is a
real income elasticity. The GDP deflator series is not used for this purpose,
since it is a local-currency index and would not be commensurate with a PPP
international-dollar series.

\textbf{Fiscal capacity.} Xu et al.\ measure fiscal capacity as total government
expenditure as a share of GDP. The corresponding World Bank series, Expense
(per cent of GDP, \texttt{GC.XPN.TOTL.GD.ZS}), covers only 76.5 per cent of the
country-years in this panel and just 91 of 162 candidate countries across all
twelve years. General government final consumption is used instead. It is the
narrower aggregate, excluding transfers, subsidies, interest and capital
formation, and is therefore a lower bound on fiscal space.

\textbf{Comparability with the reference paper.} Because the Global Health
Expenditure Database was migrated to SHA 2011 and re-estimated from 2000 onward,
the expenditure levels here are not directly comparable with those reported by
Xu et al. The elasticity is comparable, since a log-log slope estimates the same
parameter under either accounting basis.

  \item Income groups form ordered, overlapping bands, confirming that the

  \item The income gradient is stable. High income countries spend between 7.4

  \item In 2020 every group rises, the high income median moving from 7.9 to 9.0

\label{fig:burden}

Figure~\ref{fig:burden} shows the distribution of the two disease burden
measures across income groups.

\begin{itemize}
  \item TB incidence falls steeply with income, from a median of 172 per 100,000
        in low income countries to 10 in high income countries, close to an
        eighteen-fold decline.
  \item NCD mortality falls far more gently, from 24.6 to 11.5 per cent, roughly

  \item Infectious burden therefore nearly disappears in rich countries while
        chronic burden remains substantial. Tuberculosis alone would understate
        disease burden precisely where health spending is highest, which is
        why both measures are included.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{xtsum} splits each variable into a between component, the country means
$\bar{x}_i$, and a within component, $x_{it} - \bar{x}_i + \bar{x}$, where the
grand mean is added back so the two are comparable (StataCorp, 2025). Stata
reports $T = 12$ rather than an average $\bar{T}$, confirming the panel is
balanced.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Between-country variation dominates for every variable.} The
        ratio of between to within standard deviations is 12.0 for log GDP, 8.3
        for log health expenditure, 7.9 for population over 65, 6.8 for log TB
        and 5.8 for NCD mortality. Two countries drawn at random differ in
        income about twelve times more than one country does between two random
        years. In variance terms, only 0.7 per cent of the variation in income
        is within-country.

  \item \textbf{This governs what each estimator can do.} Fixed effects discards
        the between component, so it identifies the income elasticity from under
        one per cent of the variation in the regressor, while pooled OLS and
        random effects draw on both. Fixed effects will therefore return larger
        standard errors on identical data.

  \item \textbf{Government final consumption is the exception}, with a
        between-to-within ratio of only 2.7 and 12 per cent of its variance
        within-country. Fiscal shares move materially year to year, so it is the
        one regressor fixed effects can identify comfortably.

  \item \textbf{NCD mortality has the least usable time variation}, 2.9 per cent
        of its variance within-country and a within standard deviation of 1.19
        against a mean of 19.0. It is a modelled series that moves slowly, so
        fixed effects will identify it weakly despite its large cross-country
        spread.

  \item \textbf{The negative within minimum is not a negative expenditure
        share.} Subtracting the grand mean recovers the actual deviations
        (StataCorp, 2025): the within maximum of 57.305 implies a
        country exceeding its own average by $57.305 - 16.756 = 40.5$ percentage
        points, and the within minimum implies one falling 34.4 points below it.
        Both are Timor-Leste, whose government consumption ran at 103.2 per cent
        of GDP in 2010 and 28.2 per cent by 2021. The within extremes are one
        country's trajectory rather than a general feature.
\end{enumerate}

Log TB incidence has one country with zero within variation: WHO reports a
constant 221 per 100,000 for Bangladesh across all twelve years. A zero within
standard deviation means the variable is time-invariant for that country
(StataCorp, 2025), so Bangladesh contributes nothing to the TB coefficient under
fixed effects.

  \item \textbf{Fixed effects carries the largest standard error on income},
        0.102 against 0.062 pooled, exactly as the summary statistics predicted:
        it identifies the elasticity from the 0.7 per cent of income variation
        that is within-country.

  \item \textbf{Time dummies change the fixed effects results considerably,
        but barely affect pooled OLS.} Under fixed effects, population over 65 falls from
        0.023 to $-0.000$, log TB from $-0.074$ to 0.002, and NCD mortality
        loses significance. Pooled coefficients move in the third decimal. The
        reason is in Question 7: these three regressors have only 1.6, 2.1 and
        2.9 per cent of their variance within-country, and most of that little
        is a common trend shared by all countries. Once year effects absorb it,
        little identifying variation remains. Pooled OLS draws mainly on
        between-country variation, which year dummies leave untouched.

  \item \textbf{Government final consumption is the only regressor significant
        in all six models}, at 0.014 to 0.020. It is also the only one with
        substantial within-country variation, 12 per cent of its variance. A one
        percentage point rise in the government consumption share is associated
        with roughly 1.4 to 2.0 per cent higher health spending per capita.

  \item \textbf{Both disease measures carry negative signs where significant},
        which should not be read as disease reducing health spending. High TB
        incidence and high premature NCD mortality are markers of weak health
        systems and low development. Conditional on income they proxy for
        residual underdevelopment, so the negative sign reflects that
        association rather than a demand response to illness.

  \item \textbf{Income group behaves differently in each estimator.} Fixed
        effects absorbs it entirely, since it is time-invariant. Pooled OLS
        finds it insignificant, because log GDP already captures the income
        gradient. Random effects, which weights between and within variation by
        their relative variances, recovers a significant high income premium of
        0.604 once time effects are included.
\end{enumerate}

$R^2$ is not comparable across the three estimators: 0.953 for pooled is an
overall fit dominated by between-country variation, while 0.445 to 0.481 for
fixed and random effects is within-country fit.

\textbf{(f) Interpretation of the income coefficient.} In the fixed effects
model with time dummies, the coefficient on log GDP per capita is \textbf{0.744}.
A one per cent rise in real GDP per capita within a country is associated with a
0.744 per cent rise in real health expenditure per capita, holding fiscal
capacity, population ageing and disease burden constant.

Since the specification is log-log, this is the income elasticity of health
expenditure directly. It is significantly below unity: testing
$H_0 : \beta_1 = 1$ gives $F(1,144) = 6.29$, $p = 0.013$, so the null is
rejected at 5 per cent. Health expenditure grows more slowly than income.

This answers research question 1. \textbf{Health care behaves as a necessity
rather than a luxury good} in this panel: as countries grow richer, health
spending rises but its share of GDP does not rise mechanically with income. The
estimate sits at the lower edge of the 0.75 to 0.95 range Xu et al.\ report for
1995 to 2008, so their central conclusion survives re-estimation on a modern
panel that includes the COVID-19 shock.

\question{Choosing between the models}

\textbf{(a) Pooled OLS against fixed effects.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0 : u_i = 0$ for all $i$. There are no country effects and pooled OLS
        is adequate.
  \item $H_1$: at least one $u_i \neq 0$.
  \item F test on the joint significance of the 144 country effects.
        $F(144,1590) = 55.80$ without time dummies and $F(144,1579) = 59.71$
        with them, $p < 0.001$ in both.
  \item \textbf{Reject $H_0$.} Country effects are present and pooled OLS is
        misspecified.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(b) Pooled OLS against random effects.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0 : \mathrm{Var}(u_i) = 0$. There is no random country effect and
        pooled OLS is adequate.
  \item $H_1 : \mathrm{Var}(u_i) > 0$.
  \item Breusch and Pagan Lagrange multiplier test. $LM = 6302.74$ without time
        dummies and $6358.42$ with them, $p < 0.001$ in both.
  \item \textbf{Reject $H_0$.} Random effects is preferred to pooled OLS.
\end{itemize}

The null places the variance on the boundary of the parameter space, so the
statistic follows $\bar\chi^2(01)$, a fifty-fifty mixture of $\chi^2(0)$ and
$\chi^2(1)$, rather than a plain $\chi^2(1)$.

\textbf{(c) Fixed effects against random effects.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0 : \mathrm{Cov}(u_i, X_{it}) = 0$. The country effects are
        uncorrelated with the regressors, so random effects is consistent and
        efficient.
  \item $H_1 : \mathrm{Cov}(u_i, X_{it}) \neq 0$. Random effects is inconsistent
        and fixed effects is consistent.
  \item Hausman test. $\chi^2(5) = 20.84$, $p = 0.001$ without time dummies;
        $\chi^2(5) = 78.04$, $p < 0.001$ with them.
  \item \textbf{Reject $H_0$.} Fixed effects is preferred.
\end{itemize}

Income group is excluded here because the test requires both estimators to
share a coefficient vector. With time dummies Stata reports that the differenced
covariance matrix has rank 5 against 16 coefficients tested and is not positive
definite, so that statistic corroborates rather than leads. The version without
time dummies is well behaved and rejects at the 0.1 per cent level.

\textbf{(d) Comment.} All three tests select the same model. Pooled OLS is
rejected twice, so unobserved country heterogeneity is substantial, as
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} and Question 7 both show. The Hausman test then rejects
random effects: the country effects are correlated with the regressors. This
seems plausible, since health system capacity and institutional quality are
bound up with a country's income level. Regressors are usually outcomes of
choice processes, so cases where random effects is appropriate tend to be the
exception (Wooldridge, p.~493). \textbf{The preferred specification is fixed effects with time
dummies}, and its elasticity of 0.744 is the estimate that answers research
question 1.

A second consideration does not depend on any test. The units are countries,
and 145 countries with complete data are closer to the population than to a
random draw from it. Each country effect is then better treated as a separate
intercept, which is mechanically what fixed effects does. Fixed effects is
generally the more convincing estimator for policy analysis on aggregated data
(Wooldridge, p.~493).

We should not overstate the rejection. The Hausman statistic of 78.04 rejects
decisively, yet the elasticity moves only from 0.746 under random effects to
0.744 under fixed effects, against a standard error near 0.10. The test rejects
on the joint coefficient vector, but the coefficient of interest is essentially
unchanged. Random effects is still reported because fixed effects cannot
identify income group.

One limitation remains. Fixed effects removes time-invariant heterogeneity but
not simultaneity. Health expenditure and GDP are jointly determined, so log GDP
is unlikely to be strictly exogenous and the elasticity is a conditional
within-country association rather than a causal parameter. Addressing this
requires instrumenting income, which is why Xu et al.\ supplement their static
models with a Blundell and Bond dynamic estimator, and which lies outside the
estimator set this assignment specifies.
